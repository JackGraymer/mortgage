# Financing & Mortgages

Swiss mortgage lending follows a strict, conservative model. Understanding the two-pillar structure, the equity rules and the affordability test is the most important part of planning a purchase.

!!! tip "Skip to the calculators"
    If you already know the numbers, use the [Amortization Calculator](calculator.md) to see the interest, principal and monthly costs for your situation — or the [Affordability Calculator](affordability.md) to find how much you can actually borrow under the stress test.

## The two-pillar mortgage structure

Swiss mortgages are split into two "pillars":

| Pillar | % of property value | Characteristics |
| ------ | ------------------- | --------------- |
| **1st mortgage** | Up to 65% | No amortization required (interest-only) |
| **2nd mortgage** | 65% → 80% | Must be amortized within 15 years (or by retirement age 65, whichever comes first) |

The **total loan-to-value (LTV)** cannot exceed **80%** of the property value (bank lending limit). Some banks allow up to 90% LTV with additional collateral or insurance, but 80% is the standard regulatory cap.

!!! note "Why the 1st mortgage is interest-only"
    The 1st mortgage (the bottom 65%) is typically **never amortized** — you pay interest on it for as long as you own the property. Only the 2nd mortgage (the portion between 65% and 80% LTV) is paid down, usually linearly over 15 years. Many owners pay off the 2nd mortgage and then hold the property interest-only.

!!! info "Indirect amortization via Pillar 3a"
    Instead of paying down the 2nd mortgage directly, you can pay the amortization amount into a **Pillar 3a** account (or life insurance) pledged to the bank. The 2nd mortgage balance stays constant, but your pledged assets grow. At the end of the term (or retirement), the 3a assets repay the 2nd mortgage. Tax advantage: Pillar 3a contributions are deductible from taxable income (up to CHF 7,256/year for employees in 2025).

## Equity requirements

-   **Minimum equity:** 20% of the property value (purchase price or bank valuation, whichever is lower)
-   **Hard cash equity:** at least **10%** of the property value must come from liquid assets (cash, securities, gift/inheritance with proof). Pension fund withdrawals (Pillar 2) or Pillar 3a withdrawals **cannot** cover this "hard" portion
-   The remaining 10% can be financed via Pillar 2 (pension fund advance or pledge) or a Pillar 3a withdrawal
-   **Valuation gap:** Banks lend against their own valuation (often below purchase price). If the bank values the property lower, you must cover the difference in cash.

**Example — property value CHF 1,000,000:**

| Source | Amount |
| ------ | ------ |
| Hard cash (10%) | CHF 100,000 |
| Pillar 2 or 3a (10%) | CHF 100,000 |
| 1st mortgage (65%) | CHF 650,000 |
| 2nd mortgage (15%) | CHF 150,000 |

!!! warning "Valuation risk"
    If you agree to pay CHF 1,100,000 but the bank values it at CHF 1,000,000, the max mortgage is CHF 800,000 (80%). You would need CHF 300,000 equity — not CHF 220,000. Always get a bank valuation indication *before* signing a reservation.

## Affordability calculation

Banks apply a strict **affordability test**: total housing costs must not exceed **33% of gross household income** (some banks use 30–35%).

**Housing costs considered:**

-   Imputed mortgage interest at a **stress rate** (typically **5%**, even if the actual rate is lower) on the *total* mortgage amount
-   Amortization of the 2nd mortgage (linear over chosen term, typically 15 years = ~6.7% of 2nd mortgage p.a., or 1% of property value as rule of thumb)
-   Maintenance costs (typically 1% of the property value per year)
-   Heating and ancillary costs (Nebenkosten, typically CHF 3,000–5,000/year for a house)

**Example — property CHF 1,000,000, mortgage CHF 800,000 (65% 1st + 15% 2nd):**

| Cost item | Annual amount |
| --------- | ------------- |
| Interest (stress 5% on CHF 800k) | CHF 40,000 |
| Amortization (2nd CHF 150k / 15 yr) | CHF 10,000 |
| Maintenance (1% of property value) | CHF 10,000 |
| Ancillary costs (estimated) | CHF 4,000 |
| **Total** | **CHF 64,000** |

Required minimum income at the 33% ratio: **CHF 194,000 / year**.

!!! warning
    The stress test is the real constraint for most buyers. Even at a low actual rate of 2%, the bank tests you at **5% on the *full* mortgage**. A rough rule of thumb: you can typically afford a property worth about **5–6× your gross annual income** (lower if high ancillary costs or low equity).

!!! tip "Joint income"
    For married couples / registered partnerships, banks use combined gross income. For unmarried couples, some banks only count the primary earner or apply a haircut.

## Interest-rate types

| Type | Description | Typical term |
| ---- | ----------- | ------------ |
| **Fixed-rate mortgage** | Fixed interest for a defined term. Predictable costs, no rate risk. | 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15 years |
| **SARON mortgage** | Variable rate = compounded 3M SARON + bank margin (typically 0.7–1.2%). Rate adjusts quarterly. | Open-ended (3-month intervals) |
| **SARON rollover / Forward SARON** | SARON with a fixed forward component or capped rate for a period. | 2–5 years |
| **Variable mortgage** | Rate set by the bank, can change at any time. No transparency on benchmark. | Open-ended |

!!! tip
    For a primary residence, a **fixed-rate mortgage** (5–10 years) removes the biggest financial risk (rising rates). SARON makes sense when you expect rates to fall, plan to sell/amortize within a few years, or want to benefit from falling rates. Compare the *all-in* cost: SARON margin + projected SARON path vs. fixed rate.

## Using Pillar 2 and Pillar 3a

**Pillar 2 (pension fund / BVG):**

-   You can either **withdraw** funds (to buy a principal residence) or **pledge** your pension account as collateral
-   Withdrawal is tax-free at the time, but is taxed when the property is sold or when you retire (at a reduced rate, but on the full withdrawn amount plus earnings)
-   An advance withdrawal reduces your future retirement benefits (both old-age and disability/survivor pensions)
-   Both spouses' Pillar 2 savings can be used
-   **Vested benefits account (Freizügigkeitskonto):** If you left a pension fund, the vested benefits can also be used/pledged

**Pillar 3a (tied pension provision):**

-   Can be fully withdrawn for the purchase of a principal residence (owner-occupied)
-   The withdrawal is taxed separately at a reduced capital withdrawal tax rate (cantonal, typically 5–10%)
-   Can also be pledged as collateral (preferred for tax efficiency)
-   Annual contribution limit 2025: **CHF 7,256** (employees with pension fund) / **CHF 36,288** (self-employed without pension fund)
-   Indirect amortization: pay the 2nd mortgage amortization into a 3a account pledged to the bank → tax deduction + asset buildup

!!! warning "Think before withdrawing pension money"
    Withdrawing Pillar 2 money reduces your retirement capital permanently, and the tax on a later sale can be substantial (taxed as income at your marginal rate). Pledging instead of withdrawing often preserves your retirement savings and avoids immediate tax — discuss this with a specialist.

!!! tip "Indirect amortization"
    Paying the 2nd mortgage amortization into a **Pillar 3a** account (pledged to the bank) gives you a tax deduction *and* builds retirement assets. The 2nd mortgage balance stays constant, but your pledged 3a grows. At maturity, the 3a repays the 2nd mortgage. Highly recommended for high earners.
